# Winning the 2026 LPS Mill Levy Override: Research & Strategy Reference

**Prepared for Citizens for LPS — August 2026** (research synthesis from published literature; cite-checked links at bottom)
Covers: the research on why school tax measures pass or fail; campaign structure and a Sept–Nov timeline built around Colorado's all-mail ballot; messaging that works (and backfires) with suburban swing voters; coalition tactics; Colorado case studies 2023–2025; and the legal two-track (district informational vs. campaign advocacy) structure.

---

## 1. What the research says about why school tax measures pass or fail

### 1.1 The electorate you get determines the campaign you must run

- **Election timing changes the electorate more than it changes anyone's mind.** Sarah Anzia's research (*Timing and Turnout*, U. Chicago Press) shows low-turnout off-cycle elections magnify the weight of organized, motivated groups (parents, educators), while high-turnout even-year elections dilute them with voters who have no connection to the schools. **November 2026 is an even-year general election** — the LPS campaign cannot rely on the "school family" electorate; it must persuade a mass electorate that includes many childless, older, and conservative-leaning voters.
- The practitioner literature agrees: on-cycle elections help districts in supportive communities but "attract more voters unfamiliar with the local school system," and politically energized partisan turnout can *lower* passage rates (EdSource; Bridge Michigan).
- **The good news for LPS:** the district's 2018 bond (56.8%) and 2020 MLO (57.7%) both passed in even-year general elections. The even-year LPS electorate has a demonstrated ~56–58% yes ceiling when the campaign is run well. The job is to protect that coalition, not build one from nothing.

### 1.2 The Colorado pass/fail record, 2024–2025: property-tax fatigue is real but beatable

- **2024 (even year):** Only about **half of Colorado school funding measures passed (22 of 41)** — down from 90% in 2020 and 68% in 2016. Of the 22 winners, only 11 were actual tax increases; **measures framed as "no new taxes" passed easily, while nearly every failed measure required higher property taxes** (Chalkbeat Colorado). But tax-increase MLOs *did* pass in 2024 where campaigns were strong: Cherry Creek, Poudre, Harrison, Durango, Mesa 51.
- **2025 (odd year): every MLO on the ballot passed — 9 for 9**, while bonds went 6 of 9 (Colorado School Finance Project). Margins were often thin — Widefield's $5.2M MLO passed **51.7%–48.3%** and Adams 12's $39.42M MLO passed **53%–47%**. Takeaway: MLOs for teacher pay are passing in Colorado right now, but with 3–7 point margins, not the 15-point cushions of 2016–2020. Every field hour matters.

### 1.3 Trust in the district is the single biggest variable

- Magellan Strategies' Colorado 2025 Public Education Survey found a paradox that defines this race: **60% of Colorado voters support increased school funding, but only 31% believe their district manages money efficiently.** Voters trust their *local* schools far more than "the system." The two top voter priorities: **attracting/retaining quality teachers (53%) and raising teacher pay to keep up with cost of living (50%)** — exactly the LPS spending plan.
- Implication: the campaign's core job is not to prove need — most voters concede need — it is to **close the trust gap** with specificity, accountability provisions, and the district's track record of delivering on 2013 and 2018 bond promises. An Ohio study found districts that provided concrete data explaining and justifying the need were **nearly seven times more likely to pass levies** (BGSU dissertation). The cautionary tale is next door: Jeffco spent years dogged by a story that it "lost track" of a $33 million promise to voters — accountability failures compound across cycles.

### 1.4 Other research findings worth building on

- **School investment raises home values.** Cellini, Ferreira & Rothstein (*QJE* 2010) used narrow-margin bond elections and found passage causes **home prices to rise about 6%**, persisting a decade — homebuyers effectively pay **$1.50 for every $1 of school investment**. The single best-evidenced argument for non-parent homeowners.
- **Seniors are not a monolith.** Berkman & Plutzer ("Gray Peril or Loyal Support?", *SSQ* 2004) found **long-standing elderly residents are a source of *support*** — community loyalty trumps tax self-interest — while recently arrived seniors are the opposition bloc. Littleton's deep-rooted senior population is winnable through affective community ties, not spreadsheets.
- **Ballot language and tax framing matter.** Measures framed around a monthly cost per $100K of value, with itemized purposes and accountability clauses in the ballot text, outperform vague asks. The winning 2025 Colorado MLOs (Adams 12, Mancos) wrote teacher pay, safety staff, and *explicit accountability/citizen-review language* directly into the ballot question.
- **Field tactics have measured effects.** Door-to-door canvassing raises turnout ~7–10 points among those contacted (Gerber & Green). Lawn signs have a small but real aggregate effect (~1 point) — their main value in an issue campaign is **social proof and permission structure**, not persuasion.

## 2. Campaign structure and the 10-week timeline (Sept 1 – Nov 3, 2026)

### 2.1 Organization chart for a volunteer issue committee

| Role | Job |
|---|---|
| **Co-chairs** | Public face; recruit the "kitchen cabinet"; one should be a non-parent/business/senior validator, not two parents |
| **Treasurer / compliance** | TRACER registration & reports, contribution records, "paid for by" disclaimers |
| **Field director** | Voter file, canvass/phone universes, school-level captains, ballot chase |
| **Communications director** | Message discipline, earned media, social, rapid response to misinformation |
| **Endorsements/coalition lead** | Elected officials, businesses, realtors, seniors, alumni, clergy, civic clubs |
| **Fundraising lead** | Target realistic budget ($40K–$80K is typical for a suburban MLO: 2–3 mail pieces, signs, digital, printing) |
| **Data/ballot-chase lead** | Daily ballot-return matching once ballots drop |
| **School captains (one per LPS school)** | Own their building's parent network: sign locations, canvass shifts, event tabling |

The best practitioner framework is SchoolCEO's "Five Stage Bond Campaign" (from 50+ districts): (1) build the proposal *with* the community — "the process is the product"; (2) turn the proposal into a story with audience-specific messaging; (3) train advocates with palm cards and small-group "coffee talks" — "it's relationships that influence people's opinions, not information alone"; (4) execute multi-channel with rapid misinformation response; (5) deliver and report back afterward. The campaign lives in stages 3–4.

### 2.2 The Colorado all-mail reality: you are running toward October 12, not November 3

- County clerks mail ballots to **every active registered voter starting 22 days before Election Day** — roughly **Oct 12–16, 2026**.
- **Older voters return ballots first and in the greatest numbers** — voters 55+ cast the large majority of early returns. Roughly a third of ballots arrive in the last days/Election Day — those late ballots skew younger.
- **Strategic consequence:** persuasion of seniors and skeptics must be *complete before ballots drop*. A brilliant mailer landing October 25 misses half the electorate. Conversely, GOTV of low-propensity supportive voters runs through the final weekend, drop-box focused. In Jeffco 2018, the bond trailed by 8,000 votes on election night and won days later at 50.22% as late ballots broke yes — build the plan (and the election-night message) around late-breaking yes votes.

### 2.3 Week-by-week timeline

**Now – Labor Day (prep):**
- Register the committee, open the bank account, file initial TRACER report.
- Acquire the voter file; build universes: (a) strong yes/GOTV (parents of enrolled students, education-motivated voters), (b) persuasion (unaffiliated homeowners, soft Republicans, 55+ long-tenured residents), (c) leave-alone.
- If budget allows, commission a quick benchmark/message poll — even n=300 tells you which frames move persuadables.
- Lock the message triangle and print palm cards/FAQ before volunteers touch a door.
- Bank sign locations now — especially arterial-road homes and businesses.

**Weeks 1–3 (Sept 1–20) — Coalition + launch:**
- Public kickoff with a deliberately broad stage: co-chairs + a retired teacher + a small-business owner + a senior + a realtor + a recognizable Republican or unaffiliated civic figure.
- Endorsement blitz: city council members' individual endorsements, chamber(s), realtor board, PTO presidents (personal capacity), LEA, former board members from earlier winning campaigns.
- School captains recruited and trained; house-party/coffee circuit begins (10–30 people; the single highest-ROI persuasion venue).
- Canvassing of the persuasion universe begins — weekends, targeting 55+ and unaffiliated homeowner precincts FIRST (they vote first).
- Earned media: op-eds in the Littleton Independent/Centennial Citizen from non-obvious validators (senior on fixed income; conservative business owner).

**Weeks 4–5 (Sept 21 – Oct 4) — Persuasion peak:**
- Mail piece #1 (persuasion: tax impact honesty + spending specifics + accountability) hits ~Oct 1–8 so it lands just before ballots.
- Yard signs deployed en masse the last week of September (visible saturation before ballots arrive = social proof).
- Forums, Rotary/Kiwanis/senior-center presentations.
- Digital persuasion ads to the 55+ and unaffiliated universes; testimonial video content.

**Weeks 6–7 (Oct 5–18) — Ballots drop:**
- Mail piece #2 ("your ballot is arriving — here's the LPS question, vote yes") timed to land Oct 9–14.
- Treat Oct 12–16 as Election Week One: all-channel push.
- Begin daily ballot-return tracking against the voter file; **stop contacting anyone who has returned a ballot** — reallocate every hour to outstanding voters.
- Rapid-response desk live: monitor Nextdoor/Facebook; correct misinformation within hours via trusted local messengers.

**Weeks 8–9 (Oct 19 – Nov 1) — The chase:**
- Chase universes shift from persuasion to turnout: supportive households with outstanding ballots get door knocks, texts, calls.
- "Ballot party" events at breweries/coffee shops; drop-box maps in every communication.
- Final weekend: all-hands blitz on outstanding likely-yes ballots; **drop-box only messaging from Oct 27 on**.

**Nov 3 + after:**
- Election-day drop-box reminders through 7 p.m.
- Do not concede or celebrate on election night if it's close — late ballots and the cure period matter.
- Win or lose: public thank-you, and push the district to stand up the promised oversight/reporting immediately — the down payment on the *next* election.

## 3. Messaging: what works, what backfires

### 3.1 The message architecture for LPS

Lead with a **trust-and-specifics** frame, because the data says need is conceded but stewardship is doubted:

1. **"Every dollar stays in LPS schools — by law."** MLO dollars are locally raised, locally controlled, cannot be taken by the state, and don't reduce state aid. In 2026 this frame has extra power as a **contrast with Prop NN**: "Prop NN is state money with no guarantee it reaches our classrooms. This measure is the only one on your ballot where 100% of the money stays in LPS schools, controlled by people you elect and can vote out."
2. **Name the exact uses and the exact cost.** "Competitive teacher pay so LPS doesn't train teachers for Cherry Creek and Douglas County to hire away; keeping class sizes reasonable; safety staff; ending the furlough day. Cost: about **$25 per year for every $100,000 of home value** — roughly $12.50 a month on a $600,000 home." Always monthly, always per-$100K, always in the same breath as the uses. Never let opponents be the first to name the cost.
3. **"We did our part first."** LPS has already absorbed $10.6M in cuts — wage freeze, a furlough day, program reductions — before asking voters for anything. This is the credibility predicate conservative voters require. Pair with the cause: a *state* funding formula change created the gap — "Don't let the state's budget problem become our kids' problem."
4. **Track record + accountability.** LPS delivered what it promised on the 2013 and 2018 bonds and the 2010/2020 MLOs — say so with receipts. Then commit forward: annual public reporting of MLO spending, citizen oversight, independent audit. The 2025 winners wrote accountability into the ballot itself. Whatever oversight the LPS ballot language contains, make it a headline, not a footnote.
5. **Property values for non-parents.** "Strong schools are why homes here hold their value" — backed by the QJE study showing bond passage raises district home prices ~6%. Best delivered by realtors, not the campaign.
6. **Teacher retention as a quality argument, not a charity argument.** Frame around *keeping the specific teachers your kids and grandkids know*, and around competitiveness: neighboring districts have passed or are seeking compensation money; LPS cannot be the low payer in the south metro labor market.

### 3.2 Handling the hard questions (don't dodge — inoculate)

- **Declining enrollment: "Why more money for fewer kids?"** Answer head-on and early: the deficit is driven by the state formula and inflation, not enrollment; buildings, buses, safety staff, and a teacher in every classroom don't scale down one student at a time; and LPS already cut $10.6M. Never sound dismissive — this is the opposition's best argument (see Complete Colorado's framing against Jeffco — assume identical attacks on LPS).
- **"I already can't afford my property taxes."** Validate, never argue. Pivot to the small concrete number, the senior homestead exemption (65+, 10-year owner-occupants get 50% of the first $200K of value exempt) and Colorado's senior property-tax deferral program — seniors' actual increase is often lower than the headline.
- **"What about Prop NN / the state?"** "Even if NN passes, none of it is guaranteed to LPS, and it doesn't fix our gap. This is the only measure where local dollars stay in LPS schools." Do **not** attach the campaign to NN's fate or share messaging/branding with it — the answer is differentiation, not defense of NN.

### 3.3 What backfires (the research and the wreckage)

- **Threat/hostage framing** — "pass this or we cut sports/music/close schools." Voters resent being cornered; it works at most once and breeds lasting suspicion. State consequences factually once (the district's job, in its factual summary), then campaign on the positive.
- **Vagueness and jargon.** "Mills," "assessed valuation," "TABOR" — confusion is a no vote.
- **"For the kids" guilt as the whole message.** It mobilizes parents and bounces off everyone else; non-parents need the economic/community/property-value case.
- **Union-fronted or partisan-coded messaging.** The association's people-power is essential, but in a district that needs Republican and unaffiliated votes, the *messengers* must be parents, seniors, and business owners. Only 28% of Colorado Republicans believe more money improves outcomes — they can still vote yes for *their* schools, *their* teachers, *their* home values, but not for a cause coded as someone else's politics.
- **Attacking opponents or fighting every Nextdoor thread.** Correct facts calmly through trusted neighbors; never punch down at taxpayers.
- **Overpromising / sloppy stewardship stories.** Every unfulfilled promise is a 20-year liability.
- **Ignoring the tax cost and hoping nobody notices.** In 2024, virtually every Colorado measure that meant a visible property-tax increase and didn't confront that fact head-on failed.

## 4. Coalition building

- **Parent networks are the army, not the audience.** Recruit a captain per school through PTO/PTA/booster networks; captains own canvass shifts, sign placement, event tabling, and their school's Facebook groups. Sports boosters, band parents, and scout networks reach non-PTO families.
- **Seniors: the decisive early-voting bloc.** They vote first and are winnable: long-tenured seniors support schools out of community loyalty. Tactics: senior-to-senior validators (retired LPS teachers, grandparents of current students) at senior centers, churches, and 55+ communities in September; a dedicated senior one-pager covering the homestead exemption, deferral program, and the real monthly cost; invite seniors *into* the schools all September — people vote for institutions they've recently walked through.
- **Realtors are the property-value messengers.** Seek South Metro Denver Realtor Association member endorsements; realtors talking to their own client lists reach exactly the non-parent homeowner persuasion universe.
- **Local business & chambers.** Workforce-pipeline and "strong schools = strong Main Street" framing; window signs on Main Street Littleton and along Broadway/Arapahoe Road; business-owner op-eds.
- **Alumni.** LPS's multi-generation identity (Littleton, Heritage, Arapahoe HS) is an asset most districts lack: "I graduated from Arapahoe, my kids did too — vote yes" testimonials.
- **Teachers' association (LEA):** the engine room — member-to-member contact, canvass and phone shifts, sign distribution — coordinated with the issue committee like any coalition partner. Rules: all activity on personal time with no district resources, and the public face stays community-first. The most persuasive educator messenger is an individual beloved teacher, not the association logo.
- **Electeds and civic validators.** Individual city council members (Littleton and Centennial), county commissioners, former school board members from past winning campaigns, and at least one visible Republican or conservative unaffiliated co-chair-level surrogate.

## 5. Colorado case studies, 2023–2025: what separates winners from losers

| District | What happened | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Douglas County** (conservative suburb) | 2023: $66M MLO **passed** (9% staff raises) while the $484M bond **failed** narrowly; 2024: rebuilt bond **passed with 60%** after the district made consequences concrete | Even deep-red suburbs pass teacher-pay MLOs; a loss can be reversed in 12 months when the ask is retooled |
| **Cherry Creek** | 2024 (even year, peak tax fatigue): $950M bond ~56% and $9M MLO ~55%, both **passed** | A high-trust brand + perennial campaign infrastructure beats a bad environment; LPS's closest analog |
| **Thompson (Loveland)** | 2024 bond **failed** (trust deficit); 2025: $99M bond **passed 59–41** by pivoting to a no-tax-rate-increase continuation frame and promising a Citizens' Bond Oversight Committee — written into the ballot | Trust + tax-framing are the whole ballgame; institutionalized oversight converts skeptics |
| **Brighton 27J** | Lost MLOs **seven times**, went to a 4-day week in 2018; finally **passed in 2022** with Commerce City cutting its own taxes in support | The cost of losing is measured in decades; municipal partners and dramatized stakes finally broke through |
| **Mesa 51 (Grand Junction)** | 2024: **passed** both a bond and an MLO renewal in a red county | Conservative-area wins run on specificity, renewal framing, and community trust |
| **Jeffco** | 2018: MLO 55%, bond won **come-from-behind at 50.22%** on late ballots; 2026: back with a $133M two-measure ask | Late mail ballots skew yes — never panic on election night; regional media will run "tax pile-on" stories — LPS must localize relentlessly |
| **2025 sweep vs. 2024 split** | 2025: MLOs 9/9 (several at 51–53%); 2024: 22/41 overall, failures concentrated in new-property-tax measures | Teacher-pay MLOs are passing statewide — but plan for a 3–6 point race, not a coronation |

**Common threads among winners:** honest, small-sounding tax framing delivered by the campaign before opponents define it; an itemized spending plan with accountability in the ballot language; a trusted district with visible prior belt-tightening; messengers beyond parents and teachers; and field programs synchronized to the mail-ballot calendar. **Common threads among losers:** trust deficits, vague or maximal asks, new-tax framing left unrebutted, and campaigns that talked mostly to their own base.

## 6. The two-track structure: district "informational" vs. campaign advocacy

See `colorado-compliance.md` for the full legal reference. The operating summary:

- **The district carries need and facts; the campaign carries urgency and the ask.** District: "Here is the $10.6M gap, what we cut, what the MLO would fund, what it costs, arguments for and against." Campaign: "Vote YES."
- The district may spend public funds only on a **balanced factual summary** (both sides, no conclusion); the board may adopt a position resolution but not spend on advertising it; staff and board members may advocate freely **as private citizens on personal time**.
- Coordination on *public information* is legal and normal: the campaign builds messaging from the district's published data.
- Bright lines to police internally: no campaign work product on district time/equipment; teachers get palm cards at the LEA meeting, not in school mailboxes; principals may inform, never exhort; the campaign — not the district — answers every attack. One FCPA complaint in October, even meritless, becomes the news story that eats a week. Brief every district administrator on the rules in September so opponents can't manufacture a scandal.

## 7. LPS-specific synthesis: the path to 55%

**Assets:** four straight wins at 56–60% including two even-year wins; a compact, high-identity district with deep alumni/senior roots; visible, painful cuts already made (wage freeze, furlough day) that pre-answer the stewardship question; a modest, concrete ask ($25/yr per $100K); a spending plan (teacher retention, class sizes, safety) that matches the top two priorities of Colorado voters in current polling.

**Threats:** even-year mass electorate; property-tax fatigue (2024's ~50% pass rate); Prop NN confusion and an energized anti-tax opposition; regional "tax pile-on" media coverage from Jeffco's $133M and DougCo's asks; the declining-enrollment attack.

**The five things that most determine the outcome:**
1. **Define the cost first and small** ($12.50/month on the median home, alongside exactly what it buys) before opponents define it big.
2. **Make accountability the headline** — oversight, annual reporting, and the district's delivered-as-promised record — because trust, not need, is the swing voters' question.
3. **Win seniors and unaffiliated homeowners in September**, in person and via realtor/business/senior messengers — they vote in the first week ballots are out.
4. **Separate cleanly from Prop NN**: "the only measure where every dollar stays in LPS schools."
5. **Run the calendar backwards from October 12**, then chase outstanding ballots door-by-door through November 3 — and don't call the race on election night.
